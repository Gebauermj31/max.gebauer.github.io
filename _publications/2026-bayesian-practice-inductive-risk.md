## Accessible overview

Scientific conclusions can have consequences even when the evidence is uncertain. Philosophers describe the resulting problem as *inductive risk*: deciding whether the evidence is strong enough to accept, reject, or communicate a claim can expose people to different kinds of error and harm.

This forthcoming paper examines why that problem persists in Bayesian practice. Bayesian methods provide a powerful formal language for learning from evidence, but applying them still requires choices about models, assumptions, thresholds, losses, and decisions. The philosophical question is not simply whether Bayesian inference is coherent; it is how the surrounding practice handles the consequences of being wrong.
